Wearable AAC · context-driven vocabulary

The right word,
already on the board.

People who use AAC boards to speak are limited to whatever someone programmed in advance. VoiceLink is a body-worn device that senses the room and the conversation, and rewrites the board in about 10 milliseconds so the words that fit this moment are already there.

Scene
Heard

Four slots are pinned forever — yes / no / I need help / wait — so a bad generation can never strand someone without them. The other six are chosen per moment from a 95-word vocabulary. The teal tile is the word no pre-programmed board would have had ready.

Architecture

Three devices, one seam

A wearable senses. A Mac thinks. A tablet speaks. The wearable does no processing at all, and the web app does no processing at all — both reach intelligence only by asking the Mac.

That middle box is the whole design. Every model call in the system passes through one function with a fixed signature:

rank_vocabulary(scene, transcript, context) -> ranked_words

Because nothing else ever touches a model, the entire inference stack can be replaced — PyTorch to OpenVINO, Apple silicon to Intel — without editing a route, the firmware, or a line of UI. That is not a refactor that happened later; it is why the Intel port was a config flag rather than a rewrite.

The loop

How a board gets made

The wearable streams VGA JPEG frames every 2 seconds and 16 kHz PCM audio in 512-sample chunks. Nothing is written to disk.

  1. Listen. Whisper base.en transcribes locally. A change-gate skips the vision model when the scene hasn't moved, so the camera path costs nothing while someone sits still.
  2. Look. A vision-language model writes one sentence about the scene — "a classroom, a whiteboard with a diagram".
  3. Over-generate. Rather than guessing the single likeliest reply, the ranker produces candidates spanning nine communicative functions — request, reject, comment, question, share, feeling, social, direct-attention, clarify — plus topics visible in the scene. Breadth is the goal: one of them should be the thing the person actually means.
  4. Select for spread. Maximal marginal relevance picks six that are relevant and different from each other, so the board doesn't fill with six paraphrases of one answer. When someone has just asked a question, answer candidates get a reserved quota.
  5. Pin and serve. Four core words are pinned, symbols are resolved from a local pictogram set, and the board goes to the tablet over a WebSocket.

Every tap is logged with a snapshot of the scene, transcript and context that produced the board. That interaction data cannot be reconstructed after the fact, so it has been recorded since the first working UI — 121 rows so far, team members only.

The selection policy is deliberately not a model. Distillation replaces only the expensive candidate-generation step; the same symbolic MMR, answer quota and pinned core run afterwards in both the teacher and student paths. That is what makes the two comparable at all.

Distillation

A 3-billion-parameter teacher, replaced by 190 KB of weights

The teacher produces good boards and takes about seven seconds. That is unusable in a conversation. So the teacher's boards became a training set: 600 rows, 559 after deduplication, split into train / validation / held-out with 84 rows never seen.

The student takes a 769-dimensional context vector — sentence embeddings of the scene and transcript, plus a few flags — and emits a score per vocabulary word. Its whole job is to propose candidates fast enough that the symbolic policy can run in real time.

The speedup rests on eight teacher samples. Teacher latency drifts run to run — 7.05 s committed, 10.6 s and 11.7 s observed on a loaded machine. Quote >400× when a claim needs to be safe, and 695× only with the sample count attached.

The negative result

Why more data made agreement worse

The first student agreed with the teacher's exact word choices 23% of the time, against a target of 45%. The obvious fix was more data. More data made it worse — down to 16%, then 6.7% — while every diversity and speed gate kept passing.

The cause is arithmetic, not optimisation. The teacher speaks an open vocabulary. The student picks from a closed list of 95 words. Counting how much of the teacher's output that list can even represent:

32.5%
of teacher word slots representable at all
49.1%
of what is representable is hedge or filler
12.2%
of rows have no representable word
56.6%
ceiling even at 8.5× the vocabulary

Word-overlap was measuring the size of the label space, not the quality of the student. Adding data moved the student toward the most frequent representable words — which are disproportionately filler — so agreement fell while the boards stayed good. Coverage of intent became the gated metric and word agreement was demoted to report-only.

The fix is not a bigger list. Concrete answers are now read straight out of the scene or transcript by an open-vocabulary extractor, so a word never in the vocabulary can still reach the board. On a purpose-built set of 60 where/who/which/colour questions whose answer is present in the context, it fires on 88.3% and lands the right answer on the board 81.7% of the time — against 1.7% for the closed vocabulary alone.

This is the finding one of the three papers is built around: a closed-vocabulary student cannot be evaluated by agreement with an open-vocabulary teacher, and the usual instinct — scale the data — makes the metric worse while the system gets no worse.

Deployment

Intel and OpenVINO

The seam exists so the inference backend can move. It has: the student exports to ONNX, converts to OpenVINO IR at 1.43e-6 logit parity, and quantizes to INT8 with NNCF calibrated on 360 real feature vectors — not random noise. A vocabulary hash ships beside every artifact, so an IR built against a different word list fails loudly instead of silently serving the wrong words.

The result that holds up

The same unmodified IR was run on an Intel Core Ultra 9 185H. Compared against PyTorch on the same machine, the OpenVINO runtime inverts by CPU vendor:

Student head, 500 iterationsPyTorchOpenVINO FP32Result
Apple M4 (ARM plugin)0.015 ms0.025 ms1.7× slower
Core Ultra 9 185H0.1075 ms0.0404 ms2.7× faster

That is a within-machine ratio, so it is immune to the OS and version differences between the two boxes. It says the OpenVINO toolchain is doing genuinely Intel-specific work, and that the ARM figures elsewhere are a portability check rather than deployment performance.

Where the time actually goes

The student head is about 0.1 ms of a 10 ms board. The real cost is MiniLM — a 22M-parameter transformer on the critical path. Moving it to OpenVINO is the largest single win in the project, and it reproduces everywhere it has been measured: 1.76× on the Mac, 2.02× on Intel CPU, 1.85× on the Arc iGPU.

The NPU is enumerated and does not work. Intel AI Boost is present, but both OpenVINO backends fail to compile against it: Missing upper bound for one or more nodes. The NPU compiler requires statically shaped graphs and the IR carries a dynamic batch axis so one artifact can serve both single-board inference and batched evaluation. A batch-1 static export would probably compile. It has not been done, and it is not claimed.

INT8 is reported honestly as a footprint result, not a speed one: 236 KB against 662 KB, a 2.8× reduction, while being marginally slower than FP32 on both machines.

Ethics

Privacy is in the architecture

A body-worn camera and microphone in a room with other people is the central ethical problem, and the answer is structural rather than a policy document.

  • Local only. Every model runs on the Mac. No audio, video or transcript is sent anywhere.
  • Nothing persists. Buffers are ephemeral; the privacy endpoint reports audio_video_persisted: false and no media artifacts on disk.
  • A hardware kill switch. A debounced physical button on GPIO 14 toggles capture on the device. While paused the audio DMA still drains — so timing stays clean — but nothing is converted or sent, and no frames are grabbed.
  • A visible indicator. An LED is lit whenever capture is active, as a signal to people nearby.
  • A software pause that drops incoming data before it reaches any buffer.
  • Pseudonymisation tooling for the tap log, and a proxy consent template for a guardian of someone who cannot consent for themselves.

Until written proxy consent and pseudonym handling are in place, the system is built and tested on the project team only.

Working

The full loop runs end to end — wearable feed in, scene and transcript, board through the seam, board on the tablet, tap logged with its context. The Intel port serves real boards through OpenVINO on both CPU and integrated GPU.

Known open items

  • The same-function-pair gate fails, and the fix trades against coverage of intent.
  • Coverage of intent clears its bar by a margin the harness calls inconclusive.
  • The NPU rejects the dynamic-shape IR; a static batch-1 export is untried.
  • Absolute Intel board latencies were measured on a loaded machine and are withdrawn — only the within-machine ratios are quoted.
  • The vocabulary-sweep analysis behind the ceiling result has no committed script.
  • The wearable has never been tested with a real tablet on a real network; everything above the device is proven.

On numbers. The committed JSON artifact is the authority — not any summary document. That rule exists because the summary was wrong twice: it quoted a speedup of 841× when its own cited file said 695.5×, and carried a latency table superseded two runs earlier.
