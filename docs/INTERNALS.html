00The shape of the whole thing

If you remember one sentence about VoiceLink's machine learning, make it this one: over-generate, then select.

Nearly every design decision in the repo is a consequence of that split. A generator proposes far more candidate phrases than will fit on the board — deliberately spread across different kinds of thing a person might say. Then a separate, non-learned selection step picks the seven that best cover the space of what the user might mean.

The two halves have completely different characters. The generator is expensive, stochastic, and swappable — an LLM today, a distilled MLP tomorrow, something else later. The selector is cheap, deterministic, and never changes. Everything interesting in the project comes from keeping those two things apart.

The distillation work is best understood as: replace the expensive half, keep the cheap half exactly as it was. That's unusual — normally you distil a model to reproduce its output. Here the teacher's output is a set whose value lies in its internal structure, and the finding was that a small student can't learn that structure but can learn the pointwise relevance the structure is built from.

01Embeddings are the substrate everything else stands on

An embedding turns a piece of text into a list of numbers — here 384 of them — positioned so that texts meaning similar things land near each other. "I'm tired" and "I am exhausted" end up close; "I'm tired" and "carbon dioxide" end up far apart.

VoiceLink uses all-MiniLM-L6-v2, a small sentence-transformer, through one shared loader in evaluation/embed.py. Two details there matter more than they look:

  • One model instance for the whole process. The production selector and every eval metric import the same embed(), so a metric can never be measuring a different embedding space than the thing it's measuring.
  • Vectors are L2-normalised on the way out. That means a plain dot product is cosine similarity. Every similarity in the codebase is a matrix multiply — a @ b.T — with no normalisation step to forget. This is why MMR over a 20-item pool costs microseconds.

Cosine similarity runs from −1 to 1; in practice sentence embeddings land in roughly 0 to 1. You'll see three thresholds recur: 0.85 for "these two are near-duplicates", 0.95 for "these two training rows are the same row", and 0.80 for "these two vocabulary entries are the same word".

02The query vector, and the 0.7 that stops the scene hijacking the board

Before anything is ranked, the situation is compressed into a single 384-d query vector in selection.build_query_vector. Relevance is then just cosine to this vector.

It's a weighted sum of three embedded texts:

_Q_WEIGHTS = {"transcript": 0.7, "scene": 0.2, "context": 0.1}

Those weights are a real design decision, not a default. The comment in the source says it plainly: a vivid scene must not pull the board away from the actual question. If someone asks "where do you want to sit?" in a restaurant, a scene-dominant query drags the board toward menus and reservations and away from answering the question. Vision is deliberately subordinated to speech.

There's a second subtlety. When the live path has latched an active question, that question alone becomes the transcript term — not the rolling transcript. Otherwise later small talk dilutes the question the user is still trying to answer. This is the "held obligation" idea: the turn may have closed, but the obligation to answer outlives it.

03Pool generation: forcing the LLM out of its own default failure

The expensive half is one LLM call (Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct-4bit via MLX) that returns a JSON array of candidate phrases, each tagged with a communicative function and a topic.

The prompt is slot-based, and that's the whole trick. It doesn't ask for "some good words." It demands exactly one phrase for each of nine named communicative moves, then four more on different topics — thirteen total:

1. request something          6. express a feeling
2. reject / disagree          7. be social
3. comment or react           8. direct attention
4. ask a question back        9. clarify or repair
5. share information          10-13. four different topics in play

Left to itself, an LLM asked for AAC vocabulary mode-collapses — it produces seven paraphrases of the single most likely reply. The slot structure makes spanning the space a formatting requirement rather than something you hope for. The system prompt reinforces it: "Two phrases that mean the same thing is a failure."

Two safety properties are built in below the model. A deterministic _CORE_POOL of five words is merged in before anything generated, so a bad generation can never strand the user without yes / no / help. And if fewer than four items survive parsing, a hardcoded stub list tops the pool up. The model is never load-bearing for the user's ability to say "I need help."

04MMR: the objective that makes this project a project

Maximal Marginal Relevance is a 1998 idea from document retrieval. You pick items greedily, one at a time, and each pick is scored on two things at once: how relevant it is, minus how similar it is to whatever you've already picked.

score(i) = λ · rel[i]  −  (1 − λ) · max   cos(cand_i, cand_j)
                                    j ∈ selected

With λ = 1.0 you get pure relevance — plain top-k, the mode-seeking behaviour. With λ = 0.0 you get pure spread and relevance is ignored. VoiceLink runs λ = 0.6, described in the source as "the knee."

Here's the argument that makes this more than a ranking tweak. In ordinary retrieval, a bad slate costs the user a scroll. For an AAC user under a closing conversational turn, a bad slate costs the entire turn — there is no second attempt. When the penalty for a miss is that large and recovery is impossible, maximising expected per-word accuracy is the wrong objective. You want the seven picks to cover the space, so whichever direction the user meant, a launch point is on the board.

The tie-break that does real work

MMR alone still stacks same-function words. So mmr_select takes an optional per-candidate function label and, among candidates whose scores are within TIE_EPS = 0.02, prefers one whose function hasn't been used yet. Removing this is measurable: same-function-pair rate degrades from 0.168 to 0.197.

05The core strip, and why it's four words and not five

Four phrases are pinned into every single board, in fixed order, before anything is selected:

yesnoI need helpwait

Pinning frequent "core vocabulary" is standard AAC practice and not novel. What's specific here is the choice: these four are deliberately four different communicative functions — comment, reject, request, direct-attention. Because they're always present and always functionally distinct, "this board is never mode-collapsed" becomes structurally true rather than empirically hoped-for, even if the generator fails completely.

The board is 10 slots, not 7. The comment explains why: 4 pinned + 6 dynamic leaves real room for situation-specific vocabulary. If it were 7, the safety strip would eat more than half the board.

The consequence that shows up everywhere in the metrics

Those 4 pinned words self-certify. They will always contribute functions and always partially cover intent, on every board, regardless of whether the model did anything useful. This is why the project reports a dynamic slice of every metric — scored only over the 6 slots the model actually chose. Whenever you see a full-board number that looks good (0.845 coverage) next to a dynamic number that looks worse (0.761), the dynamic one is the honest one.

Core words are also excluded from the student's learned vocabulary entirely, and re-attached deterministically at inference by assemble_board. In v1 they were in the label space, which created a 0.571 agreement floor — the student got credit for words it never had to learn.

06Answer mode: a different λ for a different job

When the system detects that the partner asked a question, the pipeline changes shape. The prompt gains an answer block, the token budget widens from 520 to 720, and selection reserves slots.

The reservation uses a lower lambda than normal fill:

ANSWER_LAM = 0.35
Reserved answer slots — diversity-heavy
FILL_LAM = 0.6
The remaining slots — the normal knee
ANSWER_SLOTS = 2
At most, and never more than k_dyn − 1

The reason is precise. Without it you get six near-duplicate answers all saying roughly "I'm seated." A low lambda forces the reserved answers to be genuinely different answers — in the back / not seated / no table — so the user is choosing between real alternatives rather than synonyms.

There's a subtlety worth knowing because it's the kind of thing that looks like a bug. On the student path, infer.py deliberately does not use low-lambda MMR for the answer quota. It uses relevance order with a near-duplicate guard instead. Why: the teacher's answer candidates are all pre-tagged as answers, so spreading them is safe. The student's pool is unfiltered vocabulary, so a low lambda picks irrelevant-but-different phrases over the actual answer. Measured on a seating probe: "no, thanks." beat "table" and "aunt."

Finally, a hedge cap. At most one phrase from the "I don't know" family reaches any board — and zero when an extracted open answer is present, on the reasoning that a hedge can never contribute dynamic coverage but does count against the same-function-pair rate, so the hedge slot is the one place an answer can be added for free.

07Communicative functions, and the bug in the ruler

"Distinct communicative functions per board" is the headline metric, so how a phrase gets labelled matters enormously. There's no classifier model — labels come from nearest-exemplar matching in the same MiniLM space.

Nine functions, each anchored by several short first-person exemplars. A board item is labelled by its single nearest exemplar, not by distance to a function's average.

That distinction is the story. The first implementation used one concatenated sentence per function and classified by nearest centroid. A centroid is a blunt instrument: the "I don't know" anchor became an attractor for any short token, so both "yes" and the genuinely correct answer "carbon dioxide" got labelled I-don't-know, and "wait" got labelled refuse.

Why this is defensible rather than embarrassing

It was a bug in the measuring instrument, and the fix was applied identically to both arms — diverse and top-k alike. That's what keeps it a correctness fix rather than tuning your own arm. If a reviewer asks whether you tuned the metric to favour your method, this is the answer, and it's in the source comments with dates.

One genuine limitation to own: the nine functions have no affirmation or direct-attention bucket, so "yes" / "okay" and "wait" / "hold on" are folded into comment. Defensible, but it is a judgement call baked into your headline number.

08What "distillation" means here, and what half of it you skipped

Knowledge distillation is the standard move of training a small fast model (the student) to imitate a large slow one (the teacher). You run the teacher over many inputs, record what it produced, and train the student on those recordings.

VoiceLink's version is deliberately partial. The teacher pipeline is generate a pool, then select from it. The student replaces only the generation step. The MMR selection, the answer quota, and the hedge cap run afterwards, unchanged, on the student's output.

This wasn't the original plan — it was forced by a measurement. The student's raw torch.topk board was found to be useless: walls of hedges and same-function filler, documented in INVESTIGATION_useless_boards.md. The obvious response is more training. What actually worked was structural: treat the student's logits as a candidate pool — top pool_mult × k_dyn = 3 × 6 = 18 — and re-run the production selection over it.

The source states the principle in one line: the trained model provides relevance candidates, the selection provides the diversity the raw argmax threw away.

Say this to a reviewer as: the learned component supplies relevance; the symbolic component supplies structure. It's distinct from "learning to rank then re-ranking," because the re-ranker here isn't learned — it's the teacher's own deterministic policy, reused verbatim.

09The student is smaller than you'd guess

LayerNorm(769)
Linear(769 → 192)
ReLU
Dropout(0.3)
Linear(192 → 94)

That's it — about 165,000 parameters, roughly 660 KB on disk. Five layers. There is no transformer here; the heavy semantic lifting already happened in MiniLM, and this head just maps a context embedding to a score per vocabulary word.

Why every layer is a boring one

This is deliberate and worth understanding, because it's the decision that made the Intel path work at all. Every operation here is in ONNX opset 17 and in the OpenVINO operator set. The comment in config.yaml is explicit: standard layers only so it exports cleanly. Pick an exotic activation or a custom op and the export silently breaks — or worse, converts but produces different numbers. The project calls this "export-first": the deployment constraint drove the architecture, not the other way round.

The 769-dimensional input, and why it isn't 384

The input is a concatenation:

[ scene embedding (384) | transcript embedding (384) | is_question (1) ] = 769

Versions 1 and 2 fed a single pooled embedding of the string "<scene> || <transcript>" — one 384-d vector for both. That averages the two into a blur, and the model has no way to weight the scene axis differently from the speech axis. Given that the teacher's own query vector weights transcript at 0.7 and scene at 0.2, the student was being denied the very distinction the teacher relies on. Splitting them is v2.2, and the reason it was done is the negative result in §12.

Multi-label, not multi-class

The output is 94 numbers, one per vocabulary word. This is a multi-label problem: the question is "which of these 94 belong on the board right now," and six of them should be yes simultaneously. That's why the output goes through a sigmoid (each word independently scored 0–1) rather than a softmax (which would force the 94 to compete for a single unit of probability, appropriate only if exactly one word were correct).

10The loss function has three parts, and two of them are fighting imbalance

The base is binary cross-entropy — for each of the 94 words independently, penalise being confident and wrong. But the label matrix is extremely sparse: 6 of 94 words are positive on a typical row, so a model that predicts "no" for everything is right 94% of the time. Two mechanisms push back.

Positive weighting

pos_weight = clip(N_neg / N_pos, 1, 20)

Per word, how many times more often it's absent than present, capped at 20×. Getting a rare word wrong hurts up to twenty times more than getting a common word wrong. The cap stops a word seen three times from dominating the gradient.

Logit adjustment

adj = logits − τ · log(prior)     # τ = 1.0

Subtracting the log of each word's base rate during training means the model can't win by simply learning which words are common — that information is already accounted for, so it has to learn something context-dependent instead.

A detail worth admiring

The adjustment is applied only inside the loss function, never at inference and never at export. The exported ONNX is the unmodified model. This is exactly the kind of thing that silently breaks a deployment when someone bakes a training-time correction into the graph — and the source comment says so explicitly.

The listwise term

loss = BCE(adjusted) + 0.3 · listwise

BCE treats all 94 words as 94 unrelated coin flips. But a board is a set, and what matters is which words beat which. The auxiliary term is a log-softmax over the 94 logits averaged across the positives — it pushes the true board words to outrank everything else as a group. Weighted at 0.3, so it nudges rather than dominates.

11The data pipeline is mostly defences against fooling yourself

559 training rows is small, and small datasets leak. The pipeline in data.py runs four filters before the train/val/test split — the ordering matters, because filtering after splitting is how leakage happens:

  1. Exact-text dedup on the context string.
  2. Category cap at 15% — no single scenario family (classroom, airport…) can dominate.
  3. Embedding dedup at cosine > 0.95. This is the important one: generated scenarios come in template families, and two near-identical contexts landing on opposite sides of the split would inflate held-out agreement for free.
  4. Split 70 / 15 / 15, seeded, and persisted to splits.json.

Then a guard most projects don't bother with: the ordered context texts are hashed into a fingerprint stored beside the split. If someone regenerates a source file and re-runs evaluation, the fingerprint mismatches and evaluation refuses to score rather than silently scoring against a stale partition. If a reviewer asks how you know your held-out numbers are real, this is the answer.

12The vocabulary, which is where all the trouble lives

The student can only output words that exist in a fixed list. That list is built by counting:

min_freq 3
a word must appear in ≥3 teacher boards to be kept
max_size 200
ceiling (only 94 survive the floor)
dedup_tau 0.80
near-synonyms collapse to one column

The dedup is cleverer than it needs to be, for a real reason. It uses single-link connected components, not greedy matching against already-kept words, because the seven hedge paraphrases only unite transitively: "i don't know" and "i am not sure" sit at 0.784 — below the 0.80 threshold — but both link through "i'm not sure" at 0.841 and 0.943. Greedy matching would have left a wall of hedges standing. Dropped variants are mapped to their representative in canonical.json so their label mass survives instead of being thrown away.

There are two guards on it: every non-hedge merge is printed for manual audit, and a non-hedge component larger than 4 raises rather than silently eating vocabulary.

13The negative result, stated mechanically

The obvious hypothesis was: the student's agreement with the teacher is poor because there isn't enough training data. The training corpus was increased fivefold. Agreement got worse: 0.232 → 0.165.

Here is the mechanism, and it's the strongest thing in the project because it's a real explanation rather than a shrug:

  1. The vocabulary is built by frequency.
  2. Concrete answers are long-tail by nature — each is correct for approximately one situation.
  3. So concrete answers rarely clear the frequency floor, and generic filler always does.
  4. Adding more teacher data adds mostly more one-off concrete words, each still below the floor — while the filler's counts keep climbing.
  5. Therefore more data pushes the vocabulary further toward filler, and the student's ceiling drops.

Measured: the 94-word vocabulary can represent only 32.5% of the teacher's word choices, and 49.1% of what it can represent is filler. Making the vocabulary eight times larger reaches only 57%.

The number that gets misread — have this answer ready

Teacher word agreement is 0.056. It sounds catastrophic. It is a ceiling artefact: the student is choosing from a 94-word list that can express a third of what the teacher says, so exact word agreement is bounded low by construction, before the model does anything. This is precisely why the project stopped grading on word-matching and grades on coverage-of-intent — does the board give the person a way to say what they mean — where the student scores 0.761 against the teacher's 0.845.

What was done about it

Not "make the list bigger" — that was measured and ruled out. The fix is architectural: answers.py extracts concrete answer spans from the context itself, so "aunt" can reach the board without ever having been in the vocabulary. Slot budgets are set per question type, because extraction confidence isn't uniform — an alternative question ("the window seat or the aisle seat?") states its own answers, while a wh-question's spans are inferred from the scene and are speculative.

Do not overstate this

The open path fires on 2 of 84 held-out rows. Its effect on the aggregate gates is statistical noise. The defensible claim is "the mechanism works on the probes and no gate regressed." Not "this improved our numbers." It didn't measurably.

The attempt that failed first, and is still in the source

CONTEXT_UNION = False is a flag over a disabled earlier approach: union single content words from the transcript and scene into the candidate pool. It failed for an instructive reason — single tokens lifted from the question have inflated cosine to the query vector, because the query is 70% that same question. Lexical self-overlap made question-echo nouns like "budget" and "doorway" outrank real answers. The ablation is stark: disabling it recovered dynamic coverage from 0.563 to 0.803.

The current open-answer path fixes both defects — it emits multi-word spans, and it never scores candidates against the query vector, ranking them against an answer-type prototype instead. Keeping the failed flag in the source so the ablation stays runnable is good practice; say so if anyone asks.

Two things about this table are worth defending out loud.

Board-variety is an anti-gaming metric. A model that outputs the same seven excellent words for every situation would score brilliantly on function diversity and near-duplicates. Board-variety catches it: 77 distinct boards out of 84 held-out rows, 0.917. Very few projects instrument against their own metric being gamed by a degenerate solution, and this one does.

Same-function-pair still fails and hasn't been softened. 0.1865 against the teacher's 0.1684, where the bar is "no worse than the teacher" and lower is better. It's survived three review rounds as a failure. Keep it that way — a project whose entire differentiator is measured honesty cannot round away its one failing gate.

The significance machinery you already have

coverage_metrics.py ships a bootstrap confidence interval over paired deltas (10,000 resamples) and a McNemar test over paired coverage flips. If a reviewer asks whether your 4.68-vs-3.88 gap is significant, you have the tooling to answer — make sure the paper actually reports the interval rather than the point estimate.

15What the evaluation does and doesn't prove

Be precise about this, because it's the first thing a good reviewer will press on.

The scenarios are generated. The judge is an LLM, labelled PROXY in the source with a hook for human comparison that hasn't been exercised. The teacher is also an LLM. So the chain is: a language model judging whether a small model imitated a language model on situations a language model invented.

The repo's own honesty note says it best — "a student that passes here may be imitating the teacher imitating GPT."

What the evaluation does establish is narrower but genuine, and you should state exactly this much:

  • Two selection policies were compared on identical inputs with an identical measuring instrument, and one dominated on every axis. That comparison is internally valid regardless of whether the scenarios are realistic.
  • The latency numbers are real measurements of real code.
  • The vocabulary-ceiling analysis is a property of the data, not of a judge's opinion — it would hold if the judge were replaced tomorrow.

What it does not establish is that any of this helps a real AAC user. There are zero human subjects, by self-imposed policy, because the wearable captures bystanders who haven't consented. Say that plainly and it reads as rigour. Let a reviewer discover it and it reads as concealment.

16ONNX → IR → INT8, and the one real finding in it

Three formats, three steps.

ONNX is a portable description of a network's computation graph — a neutral interchange format. OpenVINO IR is Intel's own runtime format, produced from the ONNX. Conversion parity is measured at 1.4 × 10⁻⁶, which means the IR computes the same numbers as PyTorch to within floating-point noise. That's a check most people skip.

INT8 quantization stores weights as 8-bit integers instead of 32-bit floats — a quarter of the bits. On disk: 661 KB → 235 KB, 2.7× smaller. But the numbers get coarser, so you have to prove the outputs didn't shift.

Calibration on real data

Quantization needs to know the realistic range of values flowing through each layer, which it learns from a calibration set. This project calibrates on 360 real 769-d feature vectors produced by the exact same row_features function used at training and inference — not random noise, which is the lazy default and gives you wrong ranges.

The finding worth writing up

Last-layer exclusion, measured

Quantizing the final vocabulary projection collapsed top-6 overlap with FP32 to 0.73. Excluding just that one MatMul — standard last-layer exclusion — restored it to 0.950, while the rest of the network stayed INT8. The exclusion is found by walking backwards from the Result node rather than by hardcoding a name, because torch's ONNX node naming isn't stable across versions.

Why it happens: that layer's outputs are logits whose relative ordering is the entire product. Everything downstream is a top-k. Quantization error that would be invisible in a hidden layer directly reshuffles the ranking here.

And the gate is honest in a way most aren't:

ok = mean_topk_overlap >= 0.8  AND  min_topk_overlap >= 0.5

The mean alone would average away a single scenario silently losing a third of its board. Gating the worst case too is the right call.

The staleness guard

Every artifact carries a vocab_sha sidecar — an order-sensitive hash of the vocabulary list. The student's outputs are positional: index 37 means whatever word is 37th. Retrain with a slightly different vocabulary and a stale IR file will happily serve confident, completely wrong words with no error. The hash makes that fail loudly at load. This is a genuinely good piece of engineering and it's the kind of detail that reads as maturity in a paper.

17The thing I'd want you to notice about your own latency

Put the two latency measurements side by side:

MeasurementValueSource

The MLP is roughly 0.06% of the board. Whatever the other ~47 milliseconds are, they are not the model you distilled and quantized.

Reading the code path, the remainder is almost certainly dominated by embed() — MiniLM encoding the ~18–24 pool candidates, plus the query, plus any newly extracted answer spans — followed by the taxonomy classification and the numpy MMR. MiniLM is a real transformer; the student is five layers of arithmetic.

This is inference from the code, not a profile — verify it

I have not measured this. Before it goes in a paper or a competition entry, profile student_dynamic_words with the embed calls timed separately. If it holds, three things follow.

One. INT8-quantizing the student cannot meaningfully improve board latency, and on ARM it's currently slower than FP32 (0.031 vs 0.025 ms). The quantization work is a legitimate demonstration of the OpenVINO lifecycle and a real size reduction — but don't let a sentence imply it made boards faster.

Two. Your actual Intel lever is MiniLM, and you already exported it — intel/artifacts/minilm-ov/ exists with tokenizer and detokenizer. It does not appear to be wired into the board path. Routing embeddings through OpenVINO would attack the part of the latency that's actually there, and it would strengthen the Intel-technologies story far more than the student quantization does.

Three. There's a cheaper win available first: the taxonomy exemplars and the 94-word vocabulary are re-embedded on paths where they could be cached once. Worth checking before you optimise anything else.

The other Intel result already in hand is the strongest one and gets undersold: whisper-base.en INT8 through OpenVINO transcribes in 0.35 s against 0.42 s for faster-whisper. That's a 70 ms saving on a component that is on the critical path, unlike the 6 microseconds saved elsewhere.

18Nine sentences you should be able to say without notes

  1. "Every model call in the system goes through one function with a fixed signature." That's why the backend swaps with a config flag, and why the latency numbers attribute to one replaceable component.
  2. "We over-generate and then select, and the selection is not learned." The generator is swappable; the selection policy is identical on every path.
  3. "Ranking by likelihood is wrong when the user gets one slate and can't recover." That's the thesis. MMR is the implementation, not the idea.
  4. "Diversity beat top-k on top-k's own metric." Exact match 0.567 vs 0.342. Spreading the board bought accuracy rather than costing it.
  5. "We distilled the generator and kept the selector symbolic, because the student couldn't learn set structure but could learn pointwise relevance."
  6. "Five-times the data made agreement worse, and we know exactly why." Frequency-built vocabulary + long-tail answers = more data drifts the vocabulary toward filler. 32.5% representable, 49.1% of that filler.
  7. "Word agreement of 5.6% is a ceiling artefact, which is why we grade coverage-of-intent instead."
  8. "One gate still fails and we report it." Same-function-pair 0.1865 vs 0.1684, through three review rounds.
  9. "Everything is synthetic, there are no human subjects, and every number is from Apple silicon." Say it before anyone asks.
