\title{One Shot at the Turn: Diversity-First Vocabulary Selection
for Real-Time Augmentative Communication}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)\\Affiliation\\\texttt{email}}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
People who cannot speak converse through augmentative and alternative
communication (AAC) boards at roughly 10--18 words per minute, against
125--185 for speech. The resulting asymmetry is not merely slow: by the time a
response is composed, the conversational turn has usually closed. We argue that
this makes AAC a real-time turn-taking problem rather than a text-prediction
problem, and that the standard objective---rank candidate words by
likelihood---is wrong for it. Under a turn deadline the user gets one slate and
cannot recover from a wrong one, so the slate should be selected for
\emph{coverage of the intent space}, not for expected per-word accuracy. We
present a wearable system that senses the conversational moment with local ASR
and a vision--language model and composes a ten-slot board---four pinned core
words and six selected per turn---through a single fixed-signature interface.
Across 120 scenarios, diversity-first composition raises distinct communicative
functions per board from 3.88 to 4.68, cuts the near-duplicate rate by
$69\times$ (0.0274 $\rightarrow$ 0.0004), and raises coverage-of-intent from
0.775 to 1.00 against a likelihood-ranked baseline. To meet the turn deadline we
distil the candidate generator into a 769-dimensional MLP and leave the
selection policy symbolic, reducing board latency from 7.05\,s to 10.1\,ms
($695\times$) while retaining 95.3\% of the teacher's functional diversity. We
report paired bootstrap intervals rather than point estimates alone, and two
gates that those intervals leave unresolved.
\end{abstract}

% =====================================================================
\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================

Work on real-time conversational agents generally asks how a \emph{machine} can
respond inside a human turn-taking window. We study the mirrored problem: a
human who cannot produce speech, and a machine whose job is to get that human's
response out before the window closes.

Roughly 1 in 31 eight-year-olds in the United States is identified with autism,
and about a quarter are nonverbal, minimally verbal, or require extensive
support \citep{cdc2025}. Many communicate through AAC boards: grids of words and
symbols that are tapped rather than spoken. Measured AAC output runs at
10--18 words per minute against 125--185 for speech
\citep{asha2020,beukelman2013}. Conversational turns in natural dialogue turn over on the
order of a few hundred milliseconds \citep{sacks1974}. The gap is not an
inconvenience; it is a structural exclusion from live conversation.

Two properties of this setting follow.

\textbf{The latency budget is set by turn-taking, not throughput.} A board that
arrives after the speaker has moved on is not a degraded answer, it is no answer.
This is a deadline, not a cost term.

\textbf{There is no second attempt.} A speaker who says the wrong word can
repair it in the next breath. An AAC user whose board offers the wrong six
words has already spent the turn. The asymmetry between a miss and a hit is
therefore substantially larger than in ranking problems where the user may
scroll, rephrase, or retry.

Together these motivate our central claim: \emph{under a turn deadline with no
recovery, ranking by likelihood is the wrong objective.} The correct objective
is to cover the space of things the user might plausibly want to do with the
turn. We formalise this as communicative-function coverage, show that a
diversity-first selection policy dominates a likelihood-ranked baseline on it,
and show that the resulting pipeline can be compressed to fit the turn window on
commodity local hardware.

Existing boards are static: a caregiver programs the vocabulary in advance and
hopes it matches the moment. Prior context-aware AAC work optimises keystroke
savings \citep{kwickchat2022,praact2024}---the right objective when composing a
sentence, the wrong one when catching a turn.

\section{System}
% =====================================================================

Three nodes. A body-worn clip built from commodity parts carries a camera and an
I2S microphone and performs \emph{no} inference; it hosts its own access point
and multiplexes tagged image and audio payloads over one persistent connection.
A nearby computer runs all perception and ranking. Any tablet renders the board
over plain HTTP and reports taps. Nothing leaves the local machine.

\textbf{Board shape.} The device displays ten slots: four pinned core words and
six recomposed each turn. Metrics score the top seven positions, four of which
are the invariant core, so all gated quantities are computed over the
\emph{dynamic} words alone and the core cannot inflate them.

\textbf{Perception.} Whisper (\texttt{base.en}) transcribes the conversation and
Qwen2.5-VL-3B \citep{qwen25vl} describes the scene, with vision gated behind
frame differencing so the audio path never blocks on it. Board regeneration is
event-driven rather than clocked.

\textbf{The seam.} Every model call in the system passes through one function
with a fixed signature,
\texttt{rank\_vocabulary(scene, transcript, context)}. The firmware never calls
a model; the tablet never calls a model. This is an engineering constraint
rather than a research contribution, but it is what allows the inference backend
to be swapped---PyTorch to OpenVINO---by configuration flag, and it is what
makes the latency numbers in \S\ref{sec:results} attributable to a single
replaceable component.

\textbf{Held obligation.} When a question is detected, the system latches an
``active question'' state with a time-to-live, consumed on tap. This exists
precisely because of the asymmetry above: the turn may close before the user
answers, and the obligation to answer outlives the turn.

% =====================================================================
\section{Selecting for coverage rather than likelihood}
% =====================================================================

Each candidate utterance is assigned one of a small set of predefined
\emph{communicative functions} (assent, refusal, request, inquiry, comment,
answer, and so on) by nearest-exemplar classification in a MiniLM
\citep{minilm} embedding space; multiple exemplars per function are required,
for reasons given in Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}.

We over-generate a candidate pool with an LLM and then select the displayed
slate with maximal marginal relevance \citep{mmr1998},
\begin{equation}
s^{*} = \arg\max_{s \in C \setminus S}
\Big[\lambda\,\mathrm{rel}(s, c) - (1-\lambda)\max_{s' \in S}\mathrm{sim}(s, s')\Big],
\end{equation}
with function labels feeding the tie-break. Four core words
(\emph{yes}, \emph{no}, \emph{I need help}, \emph{wait}) are deterministically
pinned, excluded from the learned label space, and re-attached at inference;
pinning core vocabulary is standard AAC practice, and we claim only the specific
choice and the exclusion. A reserved answer quota with a hedge cap prevents the
board from filling with non-committal filler when a question is open.

MMR is not itself novel. Our claim is that its objective is the \emph{correct} one
here, for a reason specific to turn-limited interaction, and that this is
measurable.

% =====================================================================
\section{Meeting the turn deadline}
% =====================================================================

The teacher pipeline produces a competent board in 7.05\,s (IQR 6.96--7.46,
$n{=}8$), roughly three orders of magnitude beyond any plausible turn window.
The pipeline as specified is therefore not deployable.

The distillation is deliberately partial. The student---a 769-dimensional MLP
(\texttt{LayerNorm} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{Linear} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{ReLU}
$\rightarrow$ \texttt{Dropout} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{Linear}) trained multi-label
with sigmoid + BCE over a 95-word vocabulary---replaces only the expensive
\emph{candidate generation}. Its raw top-$k$ board proved unusable, dominated by
hedges and same-function filler. Additional training did not correct this;
instead we treat the student's logits as a candidate pool
($3 \times k$) and re-run the \emph{unchanged} production MMR, answer quota and
hedge cap over it. The learned component supplies relevance; the symbolic
component supplies the diversity the raw argmax discarded.

For deployment the student exports to ONNX and converts to OpenVINO IR at
$1.4\times10^{-6}$ logit parity, then to NNCF INT8 at $2.8\times$ smaller with
mean top-6 overlap 0.950 against the FP32 head. The same unmodified IR is
portable across vendors (\S\ref{sec:limits}).

% =====================================================================
\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}
% =====================================================================

\textbf{Corpora.} The selection comparison uses 120 scenarios over five settings
(classroom, clinic, airport, dinner, direct question), 24 each, emitted
deterministically from a template generator; each carries acceptable
communicative moves authored with it. The distillation corpus is separate: 600
teacher boards reduced to 559 rows by deduplication and a per-category cap, then
split 391/84/84 by seeded permutation under a fingerprint that aborts evaluation
on drift. Of the 84 test rows, 71 are scorable for coverage.

\textbf{Metrics are deterministic.} All gated quantities are computed in a
MiniLM \citep{minilm} embedding space rather than by a language model.
Communicative function is assigned by nearest exemplar over 47 exemplars across
nine functions; items are near-duplicates at cosine ${\geq}0.85$;
coverage-of-intent is satisfied by word-boundary containment, content-token
overlap, or cosine ${\geq}0.50$ against an acceptable move. A separate LLM judge
exists in the codebase but is labelled a proxy and gates nothing. Each gate is
scored as a paired margin against its bar with a 10{,}000-resample bootstrap
interval, and passes only if that interval excludes zero. Appendix
\ref{app:protocol} gives the full protocol.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
% =====================================================================

Several of these bear directly on how the results should be read.

\textbf{Two gates are unresolved, and we round neither away.} The student places
more same-function words on a board than the teacher (0.1893 vs.\ 0.1735; the
bar is ``no worse than the teacher''), but the interval $[-0.041, +0.010]$
contains zero, so the deficit is not established at $n{=}84$; since the point
estimate is adverse we report the gate as unmet. Dynamic coverage clears its bar
by 0.0028 with an interval of $[-0.118, +0.118]$, which establishes nothing
either way, so we report it as inconclusive rather than as a pass. Roughly 0.048
of the same-function-pair rate is a fixed artefact of the pinned core.

\textbf{The evaluation is entirely synthetic.} The 120 selection scenarios are
emitted from a fixed template generator rather than transcribed from
conversation, and the teacher whose boards the student learns is itself an LLM.
A student that scores well here may be imitating the teacher imitating a larger
model. The gated metrics are deterministic, which removes judge variance but not
this dependence.

\textbf{There are no human subjects.} The wearable captures bystanders who did not consent, so testing is
restricted to the authors by self-imposed policy until written proxy consent and
pseudonym handling exist. Human validation on consented interaction logs is the
number that ultimately matters and we do not have it.

\textbf{Teacher word agreement of 0.067 reflects a representational ceiling
rather than a training failure.} The student selects from a fixed 95-word
vocabulary, and that vocabulary can
represent only 32.5\% of the teacher's word choices---49.1\% of what it can
represent is filler. Because the vocabulary is frequency-built and concrete
answers are long-tail by construction, adding teacher data pushes it
\emph{further} toward filler: a $5\times$ data increase moved agreement from
0.232 to 0.165. We report this negative result rather than the hypothesis it
falsified. This is why we grade coverage-of-intent rather than word agreement, and
why concrete answers are now read from context rather than a label space---a
mechanism that works but currently fires on only 2 of 84 held-out rows, so its
effect on the aggregate numbers is noise.

\textbf{Intel timings are ratios, not absolutes.} Run unmodified on an Intel
Core Ultra 9 185H, OpenVINO makes the student head $2.7\times$ faster than
torch; on Apple ARM the identical conversion is $1.7\times$ slower, so the ARM
figures are a portability check rather than deployment performance. That Intel
session shared the machine, so we report no absolute Intel latency. The NPU is
enumerated but rejects our dynamic-shape graph.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================

Treating AAC as a turn-taking problem rather than a prediction problem changes
the objective, and changing the objective measurably changes the board. The
argument generalises: any interface that gives a user one slate under a deadline
with no recovery path should select for coverage, not likelihood.

\section*{Ethics statement}
% =====================================================================

No human-subject data was collected: all 120 scenarios are synthetic, and no
real voice, likeness, or personal data appears in the corpora or the models. The
deployed system processes audio and video on the local machine with ephemeral
buffers, no persistent recording, and a hardware kill switch, and interaction
logs never leave the device. Because a body-worn camera and microphone capture
bystanders who have not consented, we restrict testing to the authors pending a
proxy-consent and pseudonymisation protocol.

\section*{Use of large language models}
% =====================================================================

LLMs occupy two roles here, disclosed separately.

\textbf{As components of the system under study.} A local instruction-tuned LLM
is the teacher whose boards the student distils, a local vision--language model
produces the scene description, and a local ASR model produces the transcript.
The gates themselves are computed deterministically in an embedding space, not
by a language model; the codebase contains an LLM judge, but it is labelled a
proxy and gates nothing. Every gate nonetheless depends on the teacher's
outputs, and the limitations above regarding teacher bias apply.

\textbf{As engineering assistance.} The implementation, evaluation harness and
drafts of this paper were produced with substantial AI coding assistance under a
documented multi-agent review process. All experiments were run by the authors
on the hardware described, and every number was checked against the artifact
that produced it; where an artifact is regenerable rather than committed, the
reproducing command is given in Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}. The authors take
full responsibility for the content and any remaining errors.

\section{Evaluation protocol in full}\label{app:protocol}

\textbf{Scenario corpus.} The 120 selection scenarios are produced by a
deterministic template generator over five settings---classroom, clinic,
airport, dinner, and direct question---at six templates by four fillers per
setting, giving 24 rows each. Each row carries a scene string, a transcript, and
a list of acceptable communicative moves authored alongside it. The reference
utterance used for the exact-match row of Table~\ref{tab:selection} is the first
acceptable move, and is therefore authored by the same generator that produced
the scenario rather than by an independent oracle; we report that row as
corroborative only.

\textbf{Baseline.} Both arms are invoked through the same
\texttt{rank\_vocabulary} interface at temperature 0. The diversity-first arm
generates a candidate pool under a prompt that solicits spread across
communicative functions, then pins four core words and fills six dynamic slots
by MMR. The likelihood-ranked baseline issues its own generation call under a
conventional ranking prompt and returns the model's ordered list, deduplicated
and truncated, without pinned core words. The two arms therefore differ in both
prompt and selection rule, and the comparison should be read as one between
complete composition strategies.

\textbf{Distillation corpus and split.} 600 teacher boards are reduced to 559
rows by exact deduplication on the concatenated scene and transcript, a 15\%
per-category cap, and embedding deduplication at cosine ${>}0.95$. Rows are
split 391/84/84 by a seeded permutation (seed 0, 15\% validation, 15\% test).
The split file carries a fingerprint and evaluation aborts if the corpus drifts.
Of the 84 test rows, 71 can be matched back to their acceptable-move lists and
are therefore scorable for coverage.

\textbf{Model and selection parameters.} The student is
$\mathrm{LayerNorm}(769) \rightarrow \mathrm{Linear}(769,192) \rightarrow
\mathrm{ReLU} \rightarrow \mathrm{Dropout}(0.3) \rightarrow
\mathrm{Linear}(192,95)$, trained multi-label with sigmoid and BCE. The input
concatenates a 384-dimensional scene embedding, a 384-dimensional transcript
embedding, and a binary is-question flag. MMR uses $\lambda = 0.6$ over a pool
of $3 \times k_{\mathrm{dyn}} = 18$ candidates, with two slots reserved for
answers when a question is open and a cap of one hedge per board. The board is
$k = 10$ with $k_{\mathrm{dyn}} = 6$; metrics score the top seven positions.

\textbf{Metric definitions.} All quantities are computed over
\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} embeddings, L2-normalised so that the dot product is
cosine. Communicative function is assigned by nearest exemplar over 47 exemplars
spanning nine functions (question-back, specific-answer, I-don't-know,
request-help, comment, refuse/redirect, emotion, request-clarification, social);
nearest-exemplar replaced an earlier nearest-centroid rule, and the change was
applied to both arms. Near-duplicate rate is the fraction of item pairs in the
scored window at cosine ${\geq}0.85$. Same-function-pair rate is the fraction of
pairs sharing a function label. Coverage-of-intent is satisfied when any scored
item matches any acceptable move by word-boundary containment in either
direction, non-empty content-token intersection, or cosine ${\geq}0.50$. Board
variety is the fraction of held-out rows producing a distinct board as an
unordered set. Because four of the seven scored positions are always the pinned
core, the gated coverage and same-function quantities are computed over the
dynamic words only.

\textbf{Intervals.} Each gate is evaluated as a paired per-scenario margin
against its bar---relative to the teacher for coverage and same-function-pair
rate---with a 10{,}000-resample bootstrap at seed 0 and 2.5/97.5 percentiles. A
gate passes only if the lower bound exceeds zero and fails only if the upper
bound falls below it; otherwise it is recorded as inconclusive, and a single
inconclusive margin makes the enclosing gate inconclusive.

\textbf{Latency.} Student and teacher are timed in the same process on an Apple
M4. The student figure is the median of 50 scenarios after one warm-up call and
covers the complete board path: context embedding, the MLP, pooling, answer
reservation, MMR, function classification, and core assembly. The teacher figure
is the median of eight scenarios, each one pool generation plus MMR. Teacher
latency drifts with machine load; 10.6\,s and 11.7\,s have been observed on
loaded machines, and the reported figure is from a quiet one, so the speedup
should be read as a lower bound on that hardware.

\textbf{Reproduction.} The distillation artifacts, split, vocabulary, and
results file are committed. The selection corpus and its scored output are
regenerated rather than stored; \texttt{evaluation/phase3/selftest.py}
reconstructs the 120 scenarios deterministically and rescores them, and
\texttt{evaluation/phase4/evaluate.py} reproduces Table~\ref{tab:student} from
the committed split.
