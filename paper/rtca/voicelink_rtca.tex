\title{One Shot at the Turn: Diversity-First Vocabulary Selection
for Real-Time Augmentative Communication}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)\\Affiliation\\\texttt{email}}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
People who cannot speak converse through augmentative and alternative
communication (AAC) boards at roughly 10--18 words per minute, against
125--185 for speech. The resulting asymmetry is not merely slow: by the time a
response is composed, the conversational turn has usually closed. We argue that
this makes AAC a real-time turn-taking problem rather than a text-prediction
problem, and that the standard objective---rank candidate words by
likelihood---is wrong for it. Under a turn deadline the user gets one slate and
cannot recover from a wrong one, so the slate should be selected for
\emph{coverage of the intent space}, not for expected per-word accuracy. We
present a wearable system that senses the conversational moment with local ASR
and a vision--language model and composes a seven-word board through a single
fixed-signature interface. Across 120 scenarios, diversity-first selection
raises distinct communicative functions per board from 3.88 to 4.68, cuts the
near-duplicate rate by $69\times$ (0.0274 $\rightarrow$ 0.0004), and raises
coverage-of-intent from 0.775 to 1.00 against a likelihood-ranked baseline. To
fit the turn window we distil the candidate generator into a small MLP and keep
the selection policy symbolic, reducing board latency from 7.05\,s to 10.1\,ms
(${>}400\times$) while retaining 95\% of the teacher's functional diversity. We
report a gate that still fails and an evaluation that is entirely synthetic.
\end{abstract}

% =====================================================================
\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================

Work on real-time conversational agents generally asks how a \emph{machine} can
respond inside a human turn-taking window. We study the mirrored problem: a
human who cannot produce speech, and a machine whose job is to get that human's
response out before the window closes.

Roughly 1 in 31 eight-year-olds in the United States is identified with autism,
and about a quarter are nonverbal, minimally verbal, or require extensive
support \citep{cdc2025}. Many communicate through AAC boards: grids of words and
symbols that are tapped rather than spoken. Measured AAC output runs at
10--18 words per minute against 125--185 for speech
\citep{beukelman2013}. Conversational turns in natural dialogue turn over on the
order of a few hundred milliseconds \citep{sacks1974}. The gap is not an
inconvenience; it is a structural exclusion from live conversation.

Two properties follow, and both are unusual enough to be worth stating plainly.

\textbf{The latency budget is set by turn-taking, not throughput.} A board that
arrives after the speaker has moved on is not a degraded answer, it is no answer.
This is a deadline, not a cost term.

\textbf{There is no second attempt.} A speaker who says the wrong word can
repair it in the next breath. An AAC user who is given the wrong seven words has
already spent the turn. The asymmetry between a miss and a hit is therefore much
larger than in ranking problems where a user can scroll, rephrase, or retry.

Together these motivate our central claim: \emph{under a turn deadline with no
recovery, ranking by likelihood is the wrong objective.} The correct objective
is to cover the space of things the user might plausibly want to do with the
turn. We formalise this as communicative-function coverage, show that a
diversity-first selection policy dominates a likelihood-ranked baseline on it,
and show that the resulting pipeline can be compressed to fit the turn window on
commodity local hardware.

Existing AAC boards are static: a caregiver programs the vocabulary in advance
and hopes it matches the moment. Prior context-aware AAC work optimises
keystroke savings \citep{kwickchat2022,praact2024}, which is the right objective
when the user is composing a sentence and the wrong one when the user is trying
to catch a turn.

\section{System}
% =====================================================================

Three nodes. A body-worn clip ($\sim$\$15 in parts) carries a camera and an I2S
microphone and performs \emph{no} inference; it hosts its own access point and
multiplexes tagged image and audio payloads over one persistent connection. A
nearby computer runs all perception and ranking. Any tablet renders the board
over plain HTTP and reports taps. Nothing leaves the local machine.

\textbf{Perception.} Whisper (\texttt{base.en}) transcribes the surrounding
conversation; Qwen2.5-VL-3B \citep{qwen25vl} describes the scene. Expensive
vision is gated behind cheap frame differencing so that the audio path never
blocks on a slow model call. Board regeneration is event-driven, triggered by
conversational events rather than a fixed clock.

\textbf{The seam.} Every model call in the system passes through one function
with a fixed signature,
\texttt{rank\_vocabulary(scene, transcript, context)}. The firmware never calls
a model; the tablet never calls a model. This is an engineering constraint
rather than a research contribution, but it is what allows the inference backend
to be swapped---PyTorch to OpenVINO---by configuration flag, and it is what
makes the latency numbers in \S\ref{sec:results} attributable to a single
replaceable component.

\textbf{Held obligation.} When a question is detected, the system latches an
``active question'' state with a time-to-live, consumed on tap. This exists
precisely because of the asymmetry above: the turn may close before the user
answers, and the obligation to answer outlives the turn.

\section{Selecting for coverage rather than likelihood}
% =====================================================================

Each candidate utterance is assigned one of a small set of predefined
\emph{communicative functions} (assent, refusal, request, inquiry, comment,
answer, and so on) by nearest-exemplar classification in a MiniLM
\citep{minilm} embedding space. Multiple exemplars per function are required:
a single centroid mislabelled both ``yes'' and ``carbon dioxide'' as
\emph{I-don't-know}, and fixing this changed the measured outcome.

We over-generate a candidate pool with an LLM and then select the displayed
slate with maximal marginal relevance \citep{mmr1998},
\begin{equation}
s^{*} = \arg\max_{s \in C \setminus S}
\Big[\lambda\,\mathrm{rel}(s, c) - (1-\lambda)\max_{s' \in S}\mathrm{sim}(s, s')\Big],
\end{equation}
with function labels feeding the tie-break. Four core words
(\emph{yes}, \emph{no}, \emph{I need help}, \emph{wait}) are deterministically
pinned, excluded from the learned label space, and re-attached at inference;
pinning core vocabulary is standard AAC practice, and we claim only the specific
choice and the exclusion. A reserved answer quota with a hedge cap prevents the
board from filling with non-committal filler when a question is open.

MMR itself is not new. The claim is that its objective is the \emph{right} one
here, for a reason specific to turn-limited interaction, and that this is
measurable.

\section{Meeting the turn deadline}
% =====================================================================

The teacher pipeline produces a good board in 6.71\,s (IQR 6.63--6.97). That is
roughly an order of magnitude past any plausible turn window, so the pipeline as
described cannot be deployed as described.

We distil it, but not in the usual way. The student---a 769-dimensional MLP
(\texttt{LayerNorm} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{Linear} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{ReLU}
$\rightarrow$ \texttt{Dropout} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{Linear}) trained multi-label
with sigmoid + BCE over a 95-word vocabulary---replaces only the expensive
\emph{candidate generation}. The student's raw top-$k$ board was measured to be
useless, producing walls of hedges and same-function filler. The fix was not more
training: we treat the student's logits as a candidate pool
($3 \times k$) and re-run the \emph{unchanged} production MMR, answer quota and
hedge cap over it. The learned component supplies relevance; the symbolic
component supplies the diversity the raw argmax discarded.

For deployment the student exports to ONNX and converts to OpenVINO IR with
$1.4\times10^{-6}$ parity, then to NNCF INT8 at $2.8\times$ smaller with mean
top-6 overlap 0.950 (min 0.667). An INT8 \texttt{whisper-base.en} in the same
runtime transcribes in 0.35\,s against 0.42\,s for \texttt{faster-whisper}.

Table~\ref{tab:selection} is the central result. Selecting for coverage rather
than likelihood improves every measure, including---notably---exact match to the
oracle word, which the likelihood-ranked baseline is the one optimising for.
Under a turn deadline, spreading the slate across functions is not a trade
against accuracy; on this evaluation it dominates.

turn window while retaining 95\% of the teacher's functional diversity, at the
cost of one failing gate.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
% =====================================================================

We state these prominently because several are load-bearing.

\textbf{One gate fails and we are not rounding it away.} The student stacks
slightly more same-function words than the teacher (0.1893 vs.\ 0.1735, lower is
better, bar is ``no worse than teacher''). It has been reported as failing
through three review rounds and remains so.

\textbf{The evaluation is entirely synthetic.} Scenarios are generated, the
judge is an LLM, and the teacher is itself an LLM. A student that passes here may
be imitating the teacher imitating a larger model. \textbf{There are no human
subjects.} The wearable captures bystanders who did not consent, so testing is
restricted to the authors by self-imposed policy until written proxy consent and
pseudonym handling exist. Human validation on consented interaction logs is the
number that ultimately matters and we do not have it.

\textbf{Teacher word agreement is 0.067 and this is not the failure it looks
like.} The student picks from a fixed 95-word vocabulary, and that vocabulary can
represent only 32.5\% of the teacher's word choices---49.1\% of what it can
represent is filler. Because the vocabulary is frequency-built and concrete
answers are long-tail by construction, adding teacher data pushes it
\emph{further} toward filler: a $5\times$ data increase moved agreement from
0.232 to 0.165. We report this negative result rather than the hypothesis it
falsified. It is why we grade coverage-of-intent rather than word agreement, and
why concrete answers are now read from context rather than a label space---a
mechanism that works but currently fires on only 2 of 84 held-out rows, so its
effect on the aggregate numbers is noise.

\textbf{Intel timings are ratios, not absolutes.} The same IR was run
unmodified on an Intel Core Ultra 9 185H CPU and Arc iGPU, where OpenVINO makes
the student head $2.7\times$ faster than torch; on Apple ARM the identical
conversion is $1.7\times$ slower, so the ARM numbers above are a portability
check rather than deployment performance. That Intel session shared the machine
with other work, so we report no absolute Intel latency. The NPU is enumerated
but rejects our dynamic-shape graph.

\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================

Treating AAC as a turn-taking problem rather than a prediction problem changes
the objective, and changing the objective measurably changes the board. We
believe the argument generalises: any interface that gives a user one slate under
a deadline with no recovery path should select for coverage, not likelihood.

% =====================================================================
\section*{Ethics statement}
% =====================================================================

No human-subject data was collected. All 120 evaluation scenarios are
synthetically generated; no real voice, likeness, or personal data of any
individual appears in the dataset or the models. The deployed system processes
audio and video entirely on the local machine with ephemeral buffers and no
persistent recording, and carries a hardware kill switch. Because a body-worn
camera and microphone capture bystanders who have not consented, we restrict all
testing to the authors pending a proxy-consent and pseudonymisation protocol.
Interaction logs record each tap with the context that produced it and never
leave the device.

\section*{Use of large language models}
% =====================================================================

LLMs occupy two distinct roles in this work and we disclose them separately.

\textbf{As components of the system under study.} A local instruction-tuned LLM
is the teacher whose boards the student distils; a local vision--language model
produces the scene description; a local ASR model produces the transcript; and
an LLM judge scores coverage-of-intent. Every reported gate therefore depends on
model outputs, and the limitations above about judge and teacher bias apply.

\textbf{As engineering assistance.} The implementation, evaluation harness and
drafts of this paper were produced with substantial AI coding assistance under a
documented multi-agent review process. All experimental results were executed by
the authors on the hardware described, and every number in this paper was
verified against a committed artifact before submission. The authors take full
responsibility for the content and for any remaining errors.
